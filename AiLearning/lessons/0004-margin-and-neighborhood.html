Your tangible win: reject an attractive model when its prediction mechanism conflicts with the production constraint.

Support vector machines: choose a separating boundary

A support vector machine (SVM) classifies by choosing a decision boundary with the largest possible margin: the distance from that boundary to the closest training examples on either side. Those closest examples are the support vectors; they are especially influential in positioning the boundary.

For data that is not linearly separable, a kernel can represent a nonlinear boundary without explicitly constructing every transformed feature. This flexibility is not free: kernel SVMs can be costly to train and tune as data volume grows. Feature scaling also matters because distance and margin calculations depend on feature magnitude.

Exam cue: “maximum separation from the closest points” identifies a margin-based SVM. “Closest stored examples vote” identifies k-NN.

k-nearest neighbors: defer the work until prediction

k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) stores labelled training examples. For a new case, it finds the k closest examples under a distance measure, then uses their vote for classification or their average for regression.

ChoiceEffectRisk
Small kVery local, flexible boundary.Sensitive to noise: higher variance.
Large kSmoother, more stable decision.May erase useful local structure: higher bias.
Unscaled featuresLarge-unit features dominate distance.“Nearest” may no longer mean similar.

k-NN has little conventional fitting work, but a naïve implementation compares each new case with many stored cases. It is therefore often a poor starting point when online latency is strict or the reference data is large. Validate both its quality and its actual serving latency.

What determines a prediction?A learned boundary, influenced by support vectors.The labels or values of nearby stored examples.

Neither family wins by name alone. First establish a simple credible baseline, then use representative validation data and the governing constraint—such as latency, explanation, scale, or error cost—to decide whether a more complex candidate earns its place.
